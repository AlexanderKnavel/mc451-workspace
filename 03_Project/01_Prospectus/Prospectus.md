Project Prospectus

Flame Wars in Competitive Queues: Hostile Language Categories and Voice Communication Context in Ranked Match Logs

Research questions and hypotheses. RQ1: Which categories of language, such as personal attacks, game‑mechanic complaints, or systemic harassment, appear most often in text chat logs during multiplayer matches? H1: Text chat logs from matches where voice communication has been disabled will contain more personal attacks than logs from matches where voice channels are active.

Theoretical framework. Social exchange theory sorts interactions into cost and reward evaluations, and this gives the hostility categories for RQ1. Deindividuation theory says that reduced social presence and anonymity lower self‑awareness and normative restraints. When voice communication is disabled, anonymity. If players follow that logic, H1 predicts that personal attacks will appear more often in match logs without voice communication.

The gap. The studies I annotated on gaming behavior, such as those by Kwak et al., 2015 and Shores et al., 2014 examined self‑reported player survey data or automated ban reports across broad casual populations. Neither study looked at direct text chat logs in ranked queues, so there is no comparison of how specific communication channels change hostile language types.

Method overview. The unit of analysis is a player‑generated text message sent during a ranked competitive match. I will collect 150 text chat messages from 30 ranked match transcripts recorded between September and October 2026 and I will log each match ID and collection date. Short chat messages occupy a 75‑second segment in the sizing table so 150 messages amount to three hours of single‑coder analysis. I will code each message for its hostility category—personal attack, mechanic complaint, harassment or none—and I will record voice channel status, either enabled or disabled as a looked‑up variable.

Expected contribution. This study shows whether toxic communication patterns found in gaming surveys appear in real‑time ranked text logs. The findings will inform community managers and safety designers about whether chat moderation rules should change when voice communication's absent.
